# 9. Keep going

One merged pull request is a great start. What really builds skills and reputation is becoming a regular somewhere.

## Go deeper, not wider

After your first contribution to a project, do another one there. Your second pull request is much faster: you already know the setup, the tests, the conventions and the people. By the fifth, maintainers recognise your name and review you faster.

Signs you are becoming a regular:

- Maintainers mention you on issues in your area.
- You can triage new issues: reproduce them, label them, point out duplicates.
- Other contributors ask you questions.

## Grow your contributions

A natural progression:

1. **Bug fixes and docs** with clear scope.
2. **Tests** for untested areas, and **triage** of new issues.
3. **Small features** that maintainers already agreed on in an issue.
4. **Reviews** of other people's pull requests. Ask the maintainers whether outside reviews are welcome first.
5. **Larger work**: design proposals, refactors, releases. Discuss these before writing code.

## Programs that pay or mentor contributors

These change over time, so check each program's site for current dates and eligibility:

- [Google Summer of Code](https://summerofcode.withgoogle.com): paid, mentored summer projects with open source organizations.
- [Outreachy](https://www.outreachy.org): paid internships for people from groups underrepresented in tech.
- [LFX Mentorship](https://mentorship.lfx.linuxfoundation.org): mentored projects in Linux Foundation and CNCF projects.
- [GitHub Sponsors](https://github.com/sponsors): some maintainers and contributors receive sponsorship once they are established.
- Many foundations and companies run their own programs or bounties; project READMEs usually link to them.

## Showing your work

- Pin your best contributions on your GitHub profile, or list them in a profile README.
- Link specific merged pull requests on your CV, not just your profile: "Fixed an infinite loop in the login prompt (owner/project#1234)".
- Write about what you learned. A short post about debugging a real issue in a real project is interesting to read.

## Starting your own project

When you publish something yourself, the things that made projects welcoming to you apply in reverse: a README with setup steps, a `CONTRIBUTING.md`, a license, issue labels for newcomers, and quick, kind responses to the first people who show up.

## Avoid burnout

- Contribute when it is sustainable for you. Open source is not a job unless someone pays you.
- It is fine to unassign yourself from an issue you cannot finish. Say so on the issue.
- It is fine to take breaks.

## Checklist

- [ ] I picked one project to become a regular in.
- [ ] I have a list of my merged contributions with links.
- [ ] I know which mentorship or paid programs I might apply to.
